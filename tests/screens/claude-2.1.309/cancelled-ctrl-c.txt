⏺ The Keeper's Last Light

  For forty-one years, Elias Thorne climbed the 112 iron steps of Gullhaven Light every evening at dusk. He trimmed the wick, polished the great lens, and turned the brass crank that set the beam
  sweeping across the black water. Ships passed safely because he never missed a night.

  Now his knees ached, and the mainland had sent a young automated system to replace him. It arrived on a gray Tuesday in a crate marked FRAGILE, and he hated it immediately.

  "It doesn't need you," the supply boat captain said, not unkindly.

  Elias nodded and said nothing. That evening he climbed the stairs anyway, one slow step at a time, and sat beside the lens while the sun sank into the sea.

  At dusk the automated beam flickered on, steady and precise. He watched it sweep the waves, and for the first time in years he felt nothing but tired.

  Then the fog rolled in.

  It came thick and silver, swallowing the rocks and the horizon. The new system's beam scattered uselessly against the mist. Far out, Elias heard the low, uncertain horn of a fishing trawler drifting
  toward the reef.

  He stood. His knees screamed, but he went to the old manual crank and began to turn. The lamp burned in its familiar rhythm, cutting through the fog in long, patient pulses.

  The trawler's engine slowed. Then it turned, following the light home.

  When the boat bumped safely against the harbor wall, a young deckhand waved up at the tower. Elias raised his hand in return.

  The next morning, the mainland office received a short message: Keep the old keeper on the payroll. The new light is fine. But the fog still needs a human.

  Elias kept climbing the stairs.
